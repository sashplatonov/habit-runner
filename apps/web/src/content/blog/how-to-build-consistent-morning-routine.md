A morning routine sounds simple: wake up, do the things, feel great. Yet most people abandon theirs within two weeks.

The failure isn't lack of willpower. It's over-engineering.

## The Three-Habit Rule

Most morning routines collapse under their own weight. Fifteen steps, two hours, a specific sequence — and the moment one item breaks, the whole system falls apart.

The three-habit rule says: design your morning routine around **three non-negotiable habits** and treat everything else as optional.

Three habits are:
- Easy to remember without a checklist
- Achievable even on bad days
- Resilient to travel, illness, or schedule changes

A full gym session is optional. Drinking a glass of water is not. Structure your core three accordingly.

## Anchor Habits to Existing Actions

The most durable habit placement is right after something you already do without thinking. This is called **habit stacking**.

Instead of "I will meditate at 7am," try "After I make my coffee, I will sit and breathe for five minutes before touching my phone."

The coffee triggers the meditation. If the time changes, the trigger still works.

Common morning anchors:
- After your alarm goes off
- After getting out of bed
- After using the bathroom
- After starting the kettle or coffee maker

## Track Completion, Not Duration

The biggest mistake in routine design is measuring time instead of completion.

"I exercised for 20 minutes" is less stable than "I exercised." The first fails when you have 15 minutes. The second succeeds as long as you do something.

For each habit in your routine, define the **minimum viable completion**:
- Exercise → 5 minutes of movement counts
- Journaling → one sentence counts
- Reading → one page counts

On good days, you'll do more. On rough days, you'll still complete it.

## The Freeze Day: Give Yourself a Pass

Rigid streaks create brittle routines. If you know missing one day breaks a 60-day streak, you'll either do extreme things to maintain it (like exercising sick) or quit entirely when it breaks.

A better model: allow planned freeze days. One or two per week where a miss doesn't count against your streak.

## Review Weekly, Not Daily

Daily habit checking is about execution. Weekly review is about the system.

Every Sunday, spend five minutes asking:
- Which habits are green (consistent)?
- Which are yellow (inconsistent but not abandoned)?
- Which are red (need to change the minimum or drop)?

A habit that's consistently red isn't a willpower problem — it's a design problem. Reduce the friction, lower the bar, or cut it.

## Start With Less Than You Think You Need

The instinct is to add habits until the routine "feels complete." Resist this.

Start with one habit for two weeks. Prove to yourself it works. Add a second. Let that settle. Add a third.

Three habits completed daily for a year will move the needle on your life more than fifteen habits completed sporadically.
